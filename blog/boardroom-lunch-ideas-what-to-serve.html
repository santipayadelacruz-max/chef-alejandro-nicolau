Boardroom Lunch Ideas: What to Serve and What to Avoid

By Alejandro Nicolau, Private Chef·Published 7 Nov 2026·Updated 7 Nov 2026
In this article

Key takeaways

  • Keep it light and quiet. Dishes eaten with a fork, no noisy or messy food, and nothing that leaves guests drowsy.
  • Time service to the agenda. Plan service in a break, or serve discreetly so the meeting continues.
  • Offer one dish per course that suits everyone. A strong vegetarian main avoids a separate, lesser dish.
  • Think about the room. Table size, screens and papers all limit what can be served and how.
  • Brief the chef. Tell the team who is attending, how long the meeting runs and who needs what.

The best boardroom lunch is light, quiet to eat and served without interrupting the meeting: a chilled soup or salad, a main that needs only a fork, and a fruit-led dessert. Avoid anything messy, heavily spiced or sleep-inducing, and time service to the agenda so that no one has to stop mid-discussion.

Good boardroom lunch ideas share one principle: the food should support the meeting, not compete with it. Alejandro Nicolau, a London private chef trained under Martin Berasategui, cooks lunches for boardrooms and executive dining rooms across the City, Mayfair and Canary Wharf. These are the ideas and rules that make a working lunch feel considered rather than catered.

01What should you serve at a boardroom lunch?

A boardroom lunch has to do three jobs at once: feed people properly, avoid distracting from the meeting and look considered. That points towards food that is fresh, quiet to eat and easy to hold in a short break.

Think in terms of a short menu with real quality rather than a spread. Two or three components, each cooked well and served at the right temperature, make a stronger impression than a large buffet of average dishes.

02What are good boardroom lunch ideas?

  • Chilled or warm soup, then a plated main: gazpacho or a pea and mint soup in summer, a roasted squash velouté in winter, followed by grilled fish or chicken with seasonal vegetables.
  • A composed salad with protein: for example, citrus-cured salmon with fennel and herbs, or grilled chicken with grains and a light dressing.
  • A Spanish working lunch: tortilla, jamón, grilled prawns, a simple salad and a light citrus dessert, served in small portions.
  • Grazing platters for a shorter meeting: grilled vegetables, quality charcuterie, cheese, bread and fruit, set up before the meeting starts.
  • A plant-based lunch: a full vegetable-led menu that all guests can eat, which also avoids splitting the table by diet.

For more detail on how boardroom lunches are planned and served, see boardroom lunch catering in London.

03What should you avoid serving at a business lunch?

  • Anything messy: food that drips, crumbs heavily or needs hands will distract guests and mark papers.
  • Strong smells: garlic-heavy, fried or fish-forward dishes linger in a closed room.
  • Very rich or heavy dishes: a cream-based main and a large dessert produce a flat afternoon.
  • Complicated carving or assembly at the table: it interrupts the discussion.
  • Alcohol by default: offer it only if it suits the occasion and the afternoon agenda.

These are not rules for all events; a celebratory client lunch can be more generous. They are a safe baseline for a working session.

04How should service be timed around the meeting?

Timing is the difference between a smooth lunch and a disrupted meeting. There are three common approaches.

  • Break service: the meeting pauses, guests move to a side table or sit down for a plated lunch, then return. This is the most civilised option for lunches of 45 minutes or more.
  • Discreet in-room service: food is placed quietly at the table while the meeting continues, with staff trained to work without interrupting.
  • Pre-set grazing: platters are set up before guests arrive and guests help themselves when it suits them.

Whichever you choose, agree the timings with the chef in advance, including when staff will clear. The corporate dinner etiquette guide covers what to brief the chef on.

05How do you cater for different diets in a boardroom?

A boardroom group is often mixed in diet and background, and the host rarely wants to draw attention to it. The easiest approach is to design the whole menu so that most guests can eat most dishes.

Use a vegetarian or plant-based main that is as good as the meat or fish option, and label dishes clearly. Halal-friendly, kosher-style, gluten-free and allergy needs can be planned in; cross-contact cannot be fully excluded in a shared kitchen, so severe allergies should be discussed before booking.

06How do you plan a boardroom lunch in a space without a kitchen?

Most boardrooms have no kitchen at all. A chef-led team works from a nearby pantry, a small prep area or portable equipment, and menus are designed around that.

  • Cold courses and dishes finished on site work well in small spaces.
  • Hot dishes can be brought up from a prep area or finished with portable equipment.
  • Plated service needs a clear route and a short list of what the team needs: a table, power and a place to plate.

07What are good boardroom lunch ideas by season?

The season is the easiest way to keep a boardroom lunch feeling fresh, and it lets the chef cook with ingredients at their best.

  • Spring: asparagus and pea dishes, poached salmon, herb salads and a rhubarb dessert.
  • Summer: gazpacho, grilled prawns, tomato and burrata salads and berries with cream.
  • Autumn: roasted squash, mushroom dishes, slow-cooked chicken and a baked apple or pear dessert.
  • Winter: a warming velouté, braised beef or a vegetable pie, and a citrus dessert to lift the end of the meal.

A seasonal menu also makes it easier to avoid repeating the same lunch for recurring meetings.

08How do you handle a lunch that runs over?

Meetings overrun, and a lunch that depends on exact timing becomes a problem the moment they do. Plan for it.

  • Choose dishes that hold well: cold courses and slow-cooked mains rather than anything that spoils quickly.
  • Ask the chef to hold the hot course for ten to fifteen minutes without a loss of quality.
  • Agree a single contact in the meeting who can signal when to serve.

A team used to executive lunches will handle these changes quietly and keep the food at its best.

09What should you tell the caterer before booking?

  1. The date, time and length of the meeting.
  2. The number of guests and their seniority, if relevant.
  3. Diets and allergies.
  4. The room, access and kitchen or pantry facilities.
  5. Whether service should be in a break or discreet.
  6. Any confidentiality needs.

Questions

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a good lunch to serve at a boardroom meeting?

A light, quiet-to-eat menu works best: a chilled soup or salad, a main eaten with a fork such as grilled fish or chicken, and a fruit-based dessert. Avoid messy, strongly scented or very heavy dishes.

How long should a boardroom lunch take?

A working lunch usually takes 30 to 45 minutes within a longer meeting. A plated client lunch with a break normally takes 60 to 90 minutes. Agree the timing with the chef so service matches the agenda.

What food should be avoided at business meetings?

Avoid messy, crumbly or strongly scented food, very rich dishes that cause a heavy afternoon, and anything that needs carving or assembly at the table during discussion.

Can you cater a boardroom lunch without a kitchen?

Yes. A chef-led team can cook from a nearby pantry or portable equipment, with menus designed around cold courses and dishes finished on site, so a full kitchen is not needed.

How do you cater for dietary needs at a boardroom lunch?

Design the menu so most guests can eat most dishes, include a strong vegetarian or plant-based main and label dishes. Severe allergies should be discussed before booking because cross-contact cannot be fully excluded.
