How a Tasting Menu Is Designed From Scratch

By Alejandro Nicolau, Private Chef·Published 1 Oct 2026·Updated 11 Oct 2026

A well-designed tasting menu feels effortless to eat through, but building one from scratch is a deliberate process of balancing flavour, texture, pacing and story across every course.

Starting with a concept

Every tasting menu needs a through-line — a seasonal focus, a regional influence like the dishes on the Spanish Heritage menu, or simply the client's own preferences and favourite ingredients as the starting point.

Balancing flavour across courses

No two consecutive courses should compete for the same flavour space — rich should follow light, acidity should cut through richness, and the whole sequence should build rather than repeat itself.

Considering texture, not just taste

A menu built entirely around soft, rich textures becomes tiring by course four. Good tasting menu design varies texture deliberately — something crisp, something silky, something with real bite — to keep every course interesting.

Pacing the courses

Portion sizes shrink as the number of courses grows, so guests finish the meal satisfied rather than uncomfortably full. Timing between courses also matters — enough space for conversation, without long gaps that slow the evening's energy.

Building toward dessert

The final courses should feel like a natural conclusion, not an abrupt shift — a well-designed tasting menu builds anticipation toward dessert rather than treating it as a separate, disconnected finish.

Refining based on the client

The last step is always tailoring the concept to the actual guests — dietary requirements, genuine preferences, and anything specifically requested — so the finished menu feels designed for that table, not a generic showcase.

Why Some of the Best Menus Change Right Up Until the Day

Even a carefully planned tasting menu often shifts slightly in its final days — a specific ingredient wasn't quite at its best that week, or a late piece of feedback from the client suggested a small adjustment. Treating the menu as a living document until close to the event, rather than fixing it in stone too early, is often what separates a genuinely excellent tasting menu from a merely good one.

Requesting to See a Draft Before It's Finalised

Most chefs are happy to share a draft menu for feedback before finalising it, giving you the chance to flag anything you'd like adjusted. Taking advantage of this step, rather than waiting until the night of the event, is the best way to ensure the final menu truly reflects what you had in mind.

A Craft Worth Understanding

Behind every effortless-feeling tasting menu is a genuinely deliberate design process — understanding that process is part of what makes the finished evening even more enjoyable to experience.

Why Presentation Is Considered From the Start

Plating isn't an afterthought bolted onto a finished recipe — it's considered from the earliest stages of designing a tasting menu, since the visual progression across courses matters as much as the flavour progression in creating a genuinely cohesive dining experience.

The Result of Genuine Craft

A tasting menu that feels effortless to eat through is, paradoxically, usually the product of the most deliberate, carefully considered planning — proof that great design often hides the work behind it.

The balance behind the sequence

A tasting menu works like a piece of music, with rhythm, contrast and a clear ending. Light, acidic courses open the evening, richer dishes build in the middle and a dessert closes it without feeling heavy. The chef also thinks about repeated ingredients, so that the same flavour does not appear twice by accident, and about the pace of service so that guests feel looked after without being rushed. Designing that sequence for your specific guests is where most of the planning time goes.
