What Is a Walking Dinner? How to Host One in London

By Alejandro Nicolau, Private Chef·Published 7 Nov 2026·Updated 7 Nov 2026

Key takeaways

  • Small plates, served in stages. Four to six courses, each small enough to eat standing, arriving roughly every 15 to 20 minutes.
  • Eat-with-one-hand food. Fork-only or hand-held dishes, plus somewhere to put a drink, matter more than the menu list.
  • Space and flow decide success. Room to move, places to put glasses and clear routes for staff are essential.
  • Best for 20 to 100 guests. Smaller groups can sit; larger ones need stations as well.
  • Plan staff carefully. More service staff are needed than for a seated dinner of the same size.

A walking dinner is a multi-course meal served in small portions in stages while guests stand, mingle and move between rooms or stations rather than sitting at one table. It combines the feel of a dinner with the energy of a reception, and suits 20 to 100 guests at home, in venues and at company events.

Alejandro Nicolau, a London private chef trained under Martin Berasategui, plans walking dinners for private parties and corporate events. This guide explains how the format works, what it needs and when it is the right choice.

01What is a walking dinner?

A walking dinner, sometimes called a standing dinner or a progressive dinner, is a meal of several small courses served in stages. Guests eat standing or perched, move around the space and meet different people between courses.

It sits between a canapé reception and a seated dinner. Unlike canapés, the dishes are composed and served as courses; unlike a seated dinner, no one is tied to a seat or a neighbour.

02How does a walking dinner work?

  1. Guests arrive and receive a welcome drink.
  2. A first small dish is served, often a cold starter or soup in a cup.
  3. Further courses follow every 15 to 20 minutes, building from light to rich.
  4. A dessert course or table closes the meal.
  5. Coffee, petit fours and drinks continue as the party opens up.

Service can be by tray, from stations or a mix. The chef sets the pace so that guests always have something to eat without being rushed.

03How many courses should a walking dinner have?

Four to six courses is usual, each a few mouthfuls. Fewer than four feels like a canapé party; more than six can feel long for guests who are standing.

  • Four courses: a cold starter, a warm dish, a main-style dish and a dessert. Good for a two-hour party.
  • Five or six courses: add a fish course and a palate-cleansing dish. Better for a three-hour evening.

Seasonal ingredients make the menu feel connected. A winter walking dinner might run from a roasted squash soup to a slow-cooked beef dish and a citrus dessert.

04What food works best for a walking dinner?

The test is simple: can a guest eat it with one hand, or with a fork, while holding a drink? If it needs a knife or a table, it does not belong.

  • Cups and bowls: soups, risotto, ceviche or a small rice dish served in a ceramic spoon, cup or small bowl.
  • Small composed plates: a single scallop, a lamb cutlet portion or a vegetable dish designed for a fork.
  • Hand-held pieces: small tacos, mini burgers or filled breads for lighter moments.
  • Dessert in one: a tart, a mousse in a glass or a mini pastry.

See canapé catering and buffet catering for the formats on either side of a walking dinner.

05What space and staff do you need?

The room matters more than for any other format. Guests need to stand comfortably, reach somewhere to put a glass and move without queuing.

  • Allow roughly 1 to 1.5 square metres of standing space per guest, more if there are stations.
  • Provide high tables or ledges so guests can rest plates and glasses.
  • Plan a clear service route for staff that does not cross the busiest area.
  • Plan more staff than for a seated dinner: trays, clearing and drinks all need hands.

A chef-led team will walk the space beforehand, or look at photos and a plan, and recommend the number of staff and stations.

06When is a walking dinner the right choice?

  • Company parties and launches where mingling is the point. See corporate catering.
  • Milestone birthdays and house parties with 20 to 60 guests and a home that does not seat everyone.
  • Weddings and receptions as the first stage before a seated meal.
  • Networking events where seating would limit conversation.

It is less suitable when guests are older, have mobility needs or when you want formal speeches and long conversations over the table. In those cases a seated dinner works better.

07What does a walking dinner menu look like?

Here is a five-course example for a winter evening, to show how the pacing builds.

  1. Arrival: a welcome drink and a warm croqueta or small tartlet.
  2. First course: roasted squash velouté in a small cup.
  3. Second course: cured fish on a small plate with citrus and herbs.
  4. Third course: slow-cooked beef cheek with potato purée, eaten with a fork.
  5. Dessert: a chocolate and orange pot, with coffee and petit fours to follow.

Each course is a few mouthfuls. The goal is that guests leave feeling they have eaten a proper meal, without ever having sat down.

08What are the common mistakes at a walking dinner?

A walking dinner looks relaxed, but it relies on careful planning. These are the mistakes that most often spoil it.

  • Dishes that need a knife. If it cannot be eaten with a fork or a spoon, it will frustrate guests.
  • Too long between courses. A gap of more than 25 minutes breaks the rhythm and leaves guests hungry.
  • Not enough surfaces. Guests holding a plate and a glass cannot eat; give them somewhere to put one down.
  • Too few staff. Trays and clearing need hands, and waiting guests lose patience quickly.
  • No plan for dietary needs. Each course needs a vegetarian or allergy-friendly version ready to serve.

Walk through the evening with the chef beforehand and check each point against your space. Most of these problems are easy to fix on paper and impossible to fix mid-party.

09How do you plan a walking dinner?

  1. Fix the guest number and the length of the party.
  2. Choose four to six courses and the service style: trays, stations or both.
  3. Check the space for standing room, surfaces and staff routes.
  4. Share dietary needs early.
  5. Agree timings with the chef, including when each course is served.

Questions

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a walking dinner?

A walking dinner is a meal of several small courses served in stages while guests stand and mingle rather than sit at one table. It is a cross between a reception and a seated dinner, with composed dishes eaten with one hand or a fork.

How many courses are in a walking dinner?

Usually four to six small courses, served about every 15 to 20 minutes. Four suits a two-hour party; five or six suits a longer evening. Each course is a few mouthfuls, building from light to rich.

How much space do you need for a walking dinner?

Plan roughly 1 to 1.5 square metres of standing room per guest, plus high tables or ledges for glasses and plates and a clear route for staff. Stations need extra space.

How many staff do you need for a walking dinner?

More than for a seated dinner of the same size, because trays, clearing and drinks all need hands. The chef will recommend a number after seeing the space and menu.

Can a private chef cook a walking dinner at home?

Yes. A chef and team can cook and serve a walking dinner at home for around 20 to 60 guests, planning the courses, equipment and service flow around your kitchen and rooms.
